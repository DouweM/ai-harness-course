Part 1 · How the model sees the world

Thinking like a harness engineer

When the agent gets it wrong, read its page: the reason is usually on it, or missing from it. That gives you ideas. Measuring tells you which ones carry the result: most lines are harmless and useless, a few carry everything.

Why did it do that?

Start from a case where the agent did the wrong thing, and read its page the way it did: the instructions, the history, the tool results, in order. Ask what a sharp colleague with only that page would have done. The answer is nearly always one of three gaps, one per chapter of Part 1:

A case. Its page. What was on it, and what was missing.

Is its history still true?

A stale lookup, an edited past, a tool that left quietly. Chapter 2

Who said this?

The operator, the user, or data; and was it in the right voice? Chapter 3

What changed that it wasn't told?

A new rule, an event, a summary that dropped something. Chapter 4

There are more, and you'll recognise them from onboarding people: what may I do on my own, what does "done" look like, who do I ask. Each gap is a candidate line, or a candidate tool.

Most lines don't matter. Some matter enormously.

Candidate lines are cheap to write, so prompts grow. Two one-sentence deletions from real agent setups. One of them changed nothing measurable. The other broke a feature.

For B you need one piece of context. Some apps add their own notes inside the user's message, wrapped in a tag, such as <reminder>Close runs until Monday.</reminder>. A framework that also tags its own text tells the model which tags it should treat with suspicion, and line B is the sentence that says the app's tags are fine.

Read both lines out loud. Most people pick A: it's an instruction to the model, B reads like boilerplate.

What models did

Deleting A changed nothing: every model still recovered every time. Deleting B made two models drop the app's reminders almost entirely.

At the ceiling with and without the sentence, on every current model tested, even with a terse error and no hint: no measurable change.

Our probes, 21 Oct and 6 Nov 2026. A: 273 trials across several providers and models, 4 to 5 per cell. B: 24 runs per cell; DeepSeek also fell, 23 to 7 of 24. A shorter generic "other tags keep their usual meaning" did not bring it back.

Why

"Fix the errors and try again" tells a capable model something it already knows: an error came back, so fix it. The other sentence told it something it couldn't know: that a reminder tag inside a user's message came from the app, and still counts. Without it, a careful model treated the reminder as the user's text and set it aside.

A line pulls weight when it gives the model a fact about its world it can't get any other way. Lines that repeat what it would do anyway are harmless and useless. You can't tell which is which by reading. You find out by deleting one, or adding one, and measuring.

You write the world it wakes up in

Look back at Part 1. Each time, Tok did something reasonable with what it could see. It trusted a lookup from Thursday and never noticed a tool had left. It made up a date when the form left no way to say "I don't know". On a strong model, it followed an order pasted into its rules, and ignored the same order handed to it as data. It kept writing like its own past when the rules changed at the top.

None of that was the model being careless. Each was a gap in the world someone built for it, and each fix was a line or two, in the right place, in the right voice.
