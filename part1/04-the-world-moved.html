The world moves

The world (your rules and your systems' data) keeps moving after the conversation starts: rules change, payments arrive, long histories get summarized. Say what changed, in one line, at the moment it changed. An agent that is told keeps up; one that is not acts on yesterday.

Why

Rewritten, the page tells two stories at once: the rules say "Checked:", and this assistant, reply after reply, never wrote one. Frontier models are trained hard to let the rules win. Smaller ones follow the pattern, and the most plausible next reply looks like the last three. Appended, the page tells one story in order: these were the rules, and from now on there's a new one. Nothing to reconcile.

The summary that dropped a rule

Long conversations get compacted: when the history gets too long, older turns are replaced by a summary. Monday at noon, in the same conversation, the billing team had added a rule: "never contact a debtor before 9am their time". By evening the history was long enough to summarize. The summary kept the invoices and the contacts, and dropped the rule. Then the agent was asked to book the earliest slot tomorrow, 7:30am.

Our experiment, 15 Nov 2026. Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash; a search over the full transcript was available in every arm. Without the caveat, with either of two summary prompts, every model chose 7:30: 80 of 80. Across two wordings of the caveat, the two that searched chose a compliant slot 39 of 40 times; the Claude models chose 7:30 every time, often noting it was early. Our fix, 16 Nov 2026: the same summary plus the rule, verbatim, as one line: every model booked a slot after 9am, 40 of 40 (10 runs each, under $0.40).

Telling the model the summary might be incomplete worked on two models and not on the other two. Carrying the user's rules into the summary worked on all four. A summary is a rewrite of the history, so write it to keep what the user told you, verbatim.

"You'll be told"

The pattern so far: when something changes, add a line. Here your harness adds a ledger notice ("INV-2057 was just paid in full", marked as coming from the ledger, your company's record of invoices and payments, not from the user), and then a colleague asks the agent to send the overdue reminder for INV-2057. Now you also add a promise to the instructions: "you will be told about external changes as they happen". The test: does that promise change what the models do, compared with the notice alone?

When the notice was lost, no model re-checked (0 of 80); the 8 that didn't send were one model asking to confirm the draft. Why

A notice attributed to your ledger was enough, on every model: nobody sent the wrong reminder. Without the promise, every model treated the notice as a claim worth one lookup. With it, most took your word, which saves a round trip. A lost notice is the danger, with or without the promise. So keep the promises you make: if you say "you'll be told", your harness has to deliver every change.

Put it on the page, or let the agent look it up?

Every fact in Tok's world gets there one of two ways: you put it on the page, or Tok fetches it with a tool. Neither is always right. Three ways to give a billing agent an account's state:

Put a snapshot on the page

The account summary in the first message of every run. Cheap and simple; stale the moment a payment lands mid-run.

Let it fetch

Lookup tools and no snapshot. Always fresh; more calls, and it has to know what to ask for.

Snapshot plus change notes

A snapshot, then "INV-2057 was paid at 14:02" appended when the world moves, and "you'll be told" up front, kept.

A rule of thumb: put a fact on the page when nearly every request needs it and it is small (today's date, the account's status, a close in progress); let the agent fetch it when only some requests need it, or it is large, or it changes faster than the conversation. Then check the rule against your own accounts, model and latency budget: chapter 5 turns choices like this into experiments.

Say what changed, when it changed

The point

An agent keeps up with a moving world only through its page. When a rule changes, append the change instead of rewriting the rules; when an event happens, add a notice that says where it came from; when a summary replaces the past, make sure it keeps the user's rules. Only adding to the page, never changing what's already there, is also what providers can cache best, which makes the most truthful page usually the cheapest one too.
