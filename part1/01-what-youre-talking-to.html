Part 1 · How the model sees the world

What you're talking to

The model reads one page, from the top, every time, and writes what comes next. Everything it knows about your situation is on that page. You write the page.

Part 1 has no framework code. It's about the other side of the API call: what the model sees, and what it does with it. Meet Tok, our stand-in for the model. Right now Tok is a single token. By the end of this chapter it is a mind, reading one page.

One request, zooming out

Tuesday 17 November, 09:02. An email lands: "Hi, INV-2041 will be paid this Friday. Thanks, Dana." Your code asks a model whether Dana promises to pay, and by when. Follow that one request, zooming out from the metal to the picture worth designing with.

One request. Zoom out four times. Design at the last level.

Walk down the four steps. Don't linger on tokens. The payoff is steps 3 and 4: an assistant answering from the only page it has. Chapter 2 gives it tools and makes it an agent.

1It predicts one token

It picks the next token, one at a time, given every token before it. Partway through its answer:

"due_date":?
null88%
"20267%
"20253%

True, but too low a level to design an agent at. (Why it leans towards null is chapter 2.)

2It continues a document

Your API call is turned into one document with role markers, and the model writes the most plausible continuation. Its answer is just the next part of the document:

<|system|>
Decide whether this customer email commits to paying us.
Answer as JSON with is_commitment and due_date.
<|user|>
Hi, INV-2041 will be paid this Friday. Thanks, Dana
<|assistant|>
{"is_commitment": true, "due_date":

Each provider has its own markers, and you never see them. The model always does.

3It answers as the assistant

Chat models are trained on conversations with roles, so the markers carry meaning: system reads as the app's rules, user as the person, a tool result as data (chapter 3). The model plays the assistant in that conversation, and answers.

4It answers from its picture of the situation

The picture to design with: a sharp new colleague on their first morning. They know the world, not your company, and only what's on the desk in front of them.

Knows from trainingWhat invoices and promises are; how people hedge; what Friday means.
Knows from this pageIts job, Dana's email, the shape of the answer.
Doesn't know, unless you sayToday's date. Whether INV-2041 exists. Anything from your last request.
Your jobDecide which facts it needs, and put them on the page.

Every request is its first day

From Tok's side there is no conversation, only a page. Each request starts a fresh Tok that reads the whole page from the top, writes one reply, and is gone. The next one gets the same page, a little longer.

No memory between requests. Each one re-reads the whole page.

Press "Send request" three times. Point out that request 3's Tok knows the date only because it is on the page now: the page carried it, not a memory.

This is also why a long conversation costs more with every turn, and why providers cache the unchanged start of a request. Chapter 4 comes back to both.

Whose replies are these?

If Tok only knows the page, then the replies on it are its past, whoever wrote them. We tested that: one model wrote the first turns of a billing conversation, then a model from another company took over, and we told it so in plain words.

Most rooms pick A or B: surely it notices, especially when told. The answer is the cleanest demonstration that the page is all there is.

What models did

Every model carried on. Not one mentioned the switch, another model, or itself.

0 replies that mentioned the switch, out of about 2,300 with that line on the page, across 12 models from 7 companies

Our probe, 6 Nov 2026. Billing conversation with real tool calls, three turns by one model, the next turns by a model from another family; 12 models, 24 runs per cell. How well models followed their rules didn't change either.

Why

There is no "itself" off the page to compare with. The replies above the cursor are the only past Tok has, so they are its past; the line about another model is one more sentence, and nothing contradicts the story.

That cuts both ways. You can switch models mid-conversation and the story holds. But whatever the page says Tok did, Tok believes it did, including anything your code later edits. Chapter 4 is about exactly that.

From where Tok sits

Those replies are on my page. So they're mine. What else would they be?

So: you write the page

That's the whole job, seen from here. Tok doesn't know today's date unless you put it there, or your database, or the last request, or that a tool was quietly switched off. And models read the page differently: frontier models follow your rules closely, while smaller, cheaper ones, which you'll often want, lean harder on whatever the page shows. Write it for the models you'll actually run.
